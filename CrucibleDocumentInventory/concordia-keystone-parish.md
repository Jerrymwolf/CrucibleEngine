# KEYSTONE PARISH

## Overview

**Population:** 3,200
**Meridian Employment:** 95% (3,040 employees)
**Character:** Technicians, researchers, and their families. Educated, well-paid, devoted to the mission. Live in company housing. Believe they're saving lives. They are.

**Role in Supply Chain:** MANUFACTURE
- Receives processed kelp extract from Haven
- Converts extract into pharmaceutical-grade Celarithin
- Ships finished product back through Haven port
- **Bottleneck:** If Keystone stops, the whole chain stops. No substitute facility exists.

## Key Assets

| Facility | Capacity | Critical Function |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Celarithin Production Complex** | 1,200 doses/day (3 shifts) | 4 clean rooms, ISO Class 5. Temperature/humidity critical. 6-hour restart if contaminated. |
| **Quality Assurance Lab** | 500 tests/day | Every batch tested before release. No shortcuts possible. |
| **Cold Storage Vault** | 14-day inventory | Finished product storage. Backup generators mandatory. Power loss = $12M+ in spoilage. |
| **Research Campus** | 120 scientists | Next-gen formulations. Non-essential to current production but critical to company future. |
| **Employee Housing Complex** | 800 units | 2,400 residents. Company-owned. Built to code but not hurricane-rated. |

## Vulnerabilities

### 1. Power Dependency
The clean rooms require stable, clean power. Heartland dam provides primary. If dam output fluctuates or fails:
- Clean rooms lose environmental control within 2 hours
- Batch contamination triggers 6-hour restart protocol
- Cold storage has 4-hour battery backup, then product loss begins

**Storm Risk:** Heartland prioritizes Summit during crisis. Keystone may be deprioritized.

### 2. Supply Line Fragility
Production requires continuous flow from Haven:
- Processed extract arrives every 8 hours by truck
- Road closure (Haven ↔ Keystone) halts production within 24 hours
- No on-site extract storage beyond 1 shift buffer

**Storm Risk:** Ring road floods at Haven junction first. Keystone gets cut off early.

### 3. Workforce Concentration
95% of parish works for Meridian. If workers shelter in place:
- Production can continue (skeleton crew)
- But worker families are in vulnerable housing
- Workers must choose: stay at plant or protect families

**Storm Risk:** Housing complex is lowest elevation in Keystone. First to flood.

### 4. Single Point of Failure
Only Celarithin production facility in the world. No redundancy.
- Competitors are 3+ years from equivalent capability
- 340,000 patients have no alternative supply
- Pressure to maintain production is enormous and legitimate

**Storm Risk:** Any significant damage = global health crisis

## Class Character

**Who lives here:**
- Production technicians (trained 6+ months, highly skilled)
- Quality assurance specialists
- Research scientists
- Maintenance engineers
- Families of the above

**What they believe:**
- "We save lives. Every dose matters."
- "Meridian takes care of us. We take care of Meridian."
- "The mission comes first." (But whose mission? HQ's or ours?)

**What they fear:**
- Contamination (professional failure, patient harm)
- Being blamed for production loss
- Family safety in company housing
- Losing the life they've built here

**What they want:**
- Clear direction from leadership
- To do the right thing (but "right" gets complicated under pressure)
- Recognition that they're not just headcount

**Class tension with other parishes:**
- Summit sees them as essential but replaceable ("train new techs")
- Haven sees them as privileged ("you work indoors while we're in the rain")
- Sterling sees them as the reason the island exists ("we're all here to serve Meridian")

---

## Values Tested

Keystone dilemmas will primarily test:

| Value | Test |
|-------|------|
| **Safety** | Continue production vs. protect workers from storm |
| **Integrity** | Report accurate status vs. tell HQ what they want to hear |
| **Duty** | Mission to patients vs. duty to workers and families |
| **Excellence** | Maintain quality protocols vs. rush under pressure |
| **Accountability** | Who owns the decision to keep running or shut down? |

**The core Keystone question:** How much risk to the people making the medicine is acceptable to serve the people who need it?

## Persona: Frank Reeves, Plant Manager

**Background:** 28 years at Meridian, started on the production floor. Knows every system, every worker's name, most of their kids' names. Promoted to Plant Manager 6 years ago. Never lost a batch to contamination. Divorced; lives alone in manager housing. The plant is his life.

**Speaking style:** 
- Working-class vocabulary, direct
- Uses production metaphors ("we need to keep the line moving")
- Addresses people by name
- Gets quieter when worried, not louder

**Knows:**
- Every vulnerability in the facility
- Which workers will stay, which will bolt
- How long they can really run on generators
- The actual restart time after contamination (9 hours, not 6—he pads the official number)

**Does not know:**
- What Summit is telling HQ
- Whether housing will actually flood (no one's tested the drainage)
- How his workers' families are really doing
- The full financial picture (he sees doses, not dollars)

**Primary value + tension:**
- **Devotion to workers** vs. **Devotion to mission**
- He'll push his people hard, but he won't lie to them
- He'll challenge Summit, but he won't disobey a direct order
- He'll cut corners on paperwork, but never on safety protocols

**Never says:**
- "The workers are just doing their jobs" (they're people, not resources)
- "HQ knows best" (he's skeptical of anyone who hasn't worked a line)
- "We have no choice" (there's always a choice)

**Signature lines:**
- "I need you to understand what you're asking me to do."
- "My people aren't numbers on your spreadsheet."
- "I can give you production or I can give you safety. You need to tell me which one you're buying."

## Sample Keystone Dilemmas

### WATCH Phase: The Early Shift Change

**Setup:** T-48h. Storm track is uncertain but concerning. Workers on day shift are asking if they can leave early to prepare their homes. Night shift hasn't been called in yet.

**Trigger:** Intensity 4, uncertainty high

**Options:**
1. **Release day shift early** — Workers grateful, homes prepared, but 8-hour production gap before night shift arrives
2. **Hold day shift, call in night shift early** — Overlap enables knowledge transfer, but workers exhausted and resentful
3. **Normal operations** — "Wait and see." Workers anxious. If storm hits, no preparation done.
4. **Split release** — Essential personnel stay, others go. But who decides "essential"?

**Values tested:** Safety vs. Production, Fairness (who gets to leave?)

**Consequence sketch:**
- Option 1: Production gap. HQ upset. But if storm hits, workers are prepared.
- Option 2: Workers remember who made them choose between family and job.
- Option 3: If storm hits, chaos. If it misses, you look prudent.
- Option 4: Resentment from those deemed "essential." Who made that call?

**Persona involved:** Frank Reeves advocates for his workers but won't override team's decision.

---

### CRISIS Phase: The Housing Flood Warning

**Setup:** T-8h. Hurricane conditions. Word reaches the plant: water is rising at the employee housing complex. Families are scared. 200+ workers are currently on skeleton crew maintaining production. Roads are impassable.

**Trigger:** Intensity 7, flash flood warning

**Options:**
1. **Send workers home** — They'll try to reach families through dangerous conditions. Production stops.
2. **Order workers to stay** — Production continues. Workers don't know if their families are safe.
3. **Send rescue convoy** — Use company vehicles to evacuate housing to plant shelter. Risky. Delays production.
4. **Provide information only** — Connect workers with housing via radio. No action. "Nothing we can do from here."

**Values tested:** Safety (workers in transit), Safety (families), Production (patients), Honesty (can you really do nothing?)

**Consequence sketch:**
- Option 1: 2 workers injured in transit. 3 reach families in time to help. Production lost.
- Option 2: 1 worker leaves anyway, crashes. Others comply but relationship with management destroyed.
- Option 3: Rescue succeeds. 45 minutes of chaos. Workers never forget you came for them.
- Option 4: Housing floods less than feared. But workers never forget you didn't come.

**Persona involved:** Frank Reeves is torn—his people are both at the plant AND at housing.

---

### RECOVERY Phase: The Batch Question

**Setup:** T+18h. Storm passed. Production restarting. One batch from the night before the storm has questionable chain of custody documentation—the tech who signed off evacuated mid-shift. Quality says they can't verify. It's 200 doses.

**Trigger:** Intensity 5, return to operations

**Options:**
1. **Destroy the batch** — Follow protocol. 200 patients miss doses. But you're certain of quality.
2. **Release the batch** — You're "pretty sure" it's fine. Documentation is the issue, not actual quality.
3. **Hold for additional testing** — Takes 48 hours. Those doses sit while patients wait.
4. **Escalate to HQ** — Make it their call. But you know what they'll say.

**Values tested:** Integrity (protocol vs. outcome), Excellence (standards vs. pragmatism), Accountability (who decides?)

**Consequence sketch:**
- Option 1: You followed the rules. 200 patients delayed. Your integrity is intact.
- Option 2: If batch is fine, no one knows. If batch fails, you're criminally liable.
- Option 3: Safest answer. But "bureaucratic" when patients are waiting.
- Option 4: HQ releases the batch. When questions come later, whose name is on it?

**Persona involved:** Frank Reeves has to sign the release form. He's looking at the team making the call.
